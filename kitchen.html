The two problems this plan is actually solving

Everything else is just cooking. These are the constraints that shaped it.

1. Paleo failed Zack for a structural reason, not a willpower one

Whole-food, unprocessed, high-volume eating is enormously filling per calorie. That is the entire point of it, and it is exactly why it works for fat loss and fails for weight gain. Asking someone to eat 3300 calories of chicken, vegetables and sweet potato is asking them to eat roughly seven pounds of food a day. Nobody wins that fight, and losing it is not a discipline problem.

So the plan keeps the whole-food character and puts back the three things paleo removed that are most useful to a man trying to gain weight while running 25 miles a week: white rice, oats, and dairy. Rice in particular is the single highest-leverage add. It is calorie-dense, almost residue-free, easy to eat in volume, and it fuels the running better than sweet potato does. Sweet potato stays, it just stops being the only starch.

The rule that makes this work: Zack does not eat more meals, he eats denser versions of the same ones. Every day has four eating occasions, same as now. What changes is that a tablespoon of olive oil goes over the vegetables, the rice portion doubles, and there is a shake with real calories in it rather than water and powder.

2. More fiber and more calories pull in opposite directions

Zack needs more fiber and he needs 3300 calories. Fiber is the thing that makes food filling, so the obvious version of "eat more fiber" is a direct tax on the calorie target.

The resolution is to be picky about where the fiber comes from. Not more broccoli, which costs almost nothing in calories and buys enormous satiety. Instead:

SourceFiber per 100 kcalUse it because
Big salad, broccoli, mixed veg9–10 gGreat fiber, terrible calorie value. Keep it, do not lean on it
Chia seed8.1 gGoes into oats and shakes where it adds fiber without adding a chewing job
Lentils, black beans6.6–7.0 gThe workhorse. Fiber, protein and carbohydrate in one, and it disappears into a rice bowl
Berries, apple4.3–4.6 gBlend them. Liquid fiber does not fill you the way whole fruit does
Avocado4.2 gFiber and 120 calories of fat in the same half. Rare combination
Oats2.7 gModest per calorie, but you eat a lot of it, so it adds up quietly

Beans and lentils are the answer to both problems at once. A cup of black beans in a rice bowl is 227 calories, 15g of protein and 15g of fiber, and it does not read as a serving of vegetables to the person eating it. That is most of why the bowls below are built the way they are.

Ramp the fiber, do not jump to it. These days land between 41 and 55g for Zack and 35 to 41g for Hannah, which is well above the usual starting point. Going from 20g to 50g overnight buys you a miserable week and a bad Saturday long run. Weeks 1–2 hold around 30g, weeks 3–4 around 38g, week 5 onward run the full plan. Scale it by holding the beans and lentils to half portions at first. And do not eat the biggest fiber meal in the three hours before Tuesday's quality run or Saturday's long run.

Olive oil is the most underrated line on that list. A tablespoon over the vegetables at both lunch and dinner is 240 calories a day, 1700 a week, and you will not notice you ate it.

Around the training

  • Tuesday quality run and Saturday long run: eat the carbohydrate, keep the fiber and fat light in the three hours before. Oats and banana yes, lentil bowl no.
  • Lift days: it matters much less than the internet says. Get the day's total right and stop optimising.
  • The shake is Zack's, and its job is calories, not protein. He is already at 180g of protein from food. The banana, milk and honey are the point.
  • Hannah does not need a shake. She hits 115g of protein comfortably from three meals and a snack. Adding one just displaces food she would rather eat.
  • Sleep is a nutrition variable. In a controlled crossover trial under mild energy restriction, short sleep shifted what participants lost: fat loss fell about 55 percent and lean mass loss rose about 60 percent, on identical calories. Small study, ten adults, but the direction is the point and it cuts both ways for a gain phase too.

Weeks 17 to 24

Hold bodyweight. From Day A, drop the 2 tbsp of honey and half a cup of rice and you land at 3047 kcal, which is maintenance. Nothing else changes. Hannah's plan does not change at all.

Sunday, ninety minutes

The prep that makes the other six days take twenty minutes each.

Cook

  1. Rice. 6 cups dry in the biggest pot you own. It reheats fine all week and it is the single thing most likely to be missing when Zack is short on calories.
  2. Two proteins. Roast a whole chicken and brown 3 lb of ground beef with onion and spice. That is lunches through Thursday.
  3. Sweet potatoes and potatoes. Roast 5 lb on two sheet pans at 425. Fifteen minutes of work.
  4. Beans and lentils. Dry is cheaper and better but canned is fine and rinsing them takes thirty seconds. Do not let this be the step that stops you.
  5. Wash and cut the veg. The reason vegetables get skipped on a Wednesday is that they are still whole and dirty.

Standing weekly list

  • Protein: 3 lb ground beef 90/10, whole chicken, 2 lb chicken thighs, 2 lb chicken breast, 1.5 lb salmon, 1 lb cod, 2 dozen eggs, Greek yogurt, whey
  • Starch: white rice, rolled oats, 5 lb sweet potatoes, 5 lb potatoes, corn tortillas, sourdough
  • Legume: black beans, lentils
  • Produce: broccoli, whatever greens look good, onions, garlic, salad, cucumber, avocados, bananas, apples, frozen berries
  • Fat and extras: olive oil, almond butter, walnuts, whole milk, cheese, honey, chia, coconut milk, 85% chocolate
Frozen berries, not fresh. Cheaper, always in the freezer, and they go into oats and shakes without anyone deciding to use them before they go off. Worth more fiber over six months than any amount of good intent about fresh fruit.

What I would not bother with

  • Tracking every day forever. Track for two weeks to calibrate your eye, then track only when the scale stalls or moves the wrong way. The plate formula is the tracking.
  • Supplements beyond the obvious. Whey because it is a convenient food, creatine at 5g a day because it is well evidenced and cheap, vitamin D if you are indoors a lot and have not had a level checked. That is the list. The rest is money spent to feel organised.
  • Cutting carbohydrate. You are running 25 miles a week between you and the whole block is aerobic. This is the wrong six months to be low carb.
  • Weighing daily and reacting to it. Weigh most mornings, use the weekly average. Zack should see about half a pound a week. A flat week is a signal to open the density bank, not to panic.
  • Chasing the tape. Waist gets measured monthly, at the navel, first thing in the morning. Zack's hard stop is 34.5 inches, which is half an inch under the 20-point line. If it gets there, the surplus ends that week regardless of what the scale says.
How the numbers were set. Energy needs from Mifflin-St Jeor with an activity multiplier of 1.7 for Zack and 1.6 for Hannah, matched to six training days a week. Macro and fiber totals for the three worked days are computed against a food composition table rather than estimated, and each day lands within three percent of its calorie target. Protein at roughly 2.1 g/kg for Zack and 1.9 for Hannah sits inside the range usually recommended for athletes in a surplus and in hard concurrent training. The fiber-per-100-kcal ranking is arithmetic off the same table.

On the sleep note. Nedeltcheva et al., Annals of Internal Medicine, 2010. Randomised two-condition crossover, ten adults, fourteen days per condition at 5.5 versus 8.5 hours under moderate caloric restriction. Small, short, and not run on athletes in a surplus, so treat the direction as the finding and not the exact numbers.

Scope. This is a training framework, not a clinical nutrition prescription. If either of you has a reason to want one, bring this to a dietitian rather than using it instead of one.
